The first time is the hardest, and the hardest part is not the heat. It is the walk down the jetty afterwards, steaming in the cold air, knowing what the lake is about to feel like.

Then you are in, and out, and something happens that regulars struggle to describe. The usual word is quiet: not the absence of noise, but the feeling that your head has finally stopped talking.

## Heat

A wood-fired sauna runs between 70 and 90 degrees. Sit on the lower bench first; heat rises, and the top bench is for people who have done this for years. Ten minutes is plenty. Throw a ladle of water on the stones if you want the wave of steam the Finns call löyly.

## Cold

The lake does the rest. Walk in, don't jump, and breathe out slowly as the water reaches your chest. Thirty seconds is a good first goal. In winter, a hole cut in the ice does the same job with more ceremony.

> Not the absence of noise, but the feeling that your head has finally stopped talking.

## Rest

This is the part people skip, and it is the point. Sit outside wrapped in a towel, drink water, and let your body settle. Ten minutes, then go back in. Three rounds is a full evening.

## A few manners

Sit on a towel. Keep voices low. Ask before adding water to the stones. And if someone offers you a bundle of birch twigs, accept it: the soft whisking on the skin is half the tradition.
